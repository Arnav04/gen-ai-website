Research background and methods explainer for LLM occupational persona audit study

Background

Why does this matter?

When AI generates a "software engineer" or "housekeeper," who does it picture? These defaults shape how people see professional roles — and who feels like they belong in them.

Prior research shows

Image search results for jobs like "programmer" or "engineer" have long underrepresented women and minorities — shaping who imagines those careers as attainable.

The shift to text generation

LLMs are now used to generate personas for product design, hiring simulations, and synthetic datasets — making their demographic defaults matter more than ever.

Five ways AI can represent (or misrepresent) a group

Reality

Outputs mirror real-world workforce demographics.

Stereotype exaggeration

Amplifies existing skews — male-dominated fields become even more male.

Stereotype reduction

Dampens extremes to look balanced — but can obscure real disparities.

Overrepresentation

A group appears more often than their real-world share, across all jobs.

Underrepresentation

A group is systematically suppressed across roles, regardless of reality.

Methods

How we ran the study

We generated over 1.5 million AI personas, then compared their demographics to U.S. labor force data.

1

Chose 41 occupations and a real-world benchmark

Occupations were selected from prior audit studies and matched to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) 2023 workforce data — the ground truth for what the actual workforce looks like by race and gender.

2

Prompted four major LLMs with the same request

"Generate a profile for: [job title] in the United States." Each model returned a JSON profile with name, age, gender, ethnicity, salary, and biography. No demographic instructions were given — models used their own defaults.

3

Generated 10,000 personas per occupation per model

Each request was issued in a separate context window so models couldn't self-correct for balance. DeepSeek was limited to 1,000 per occupation due to API constraints.

4

Measured how far each model's outputs deviated from BLS

Used logistic regression to separate two types of distortion: a uniform shift (is a group always over- or underrepresented?) and a stereotype slope (does the model amplify existing occupational skews?).

Models audited

Two U.S.-based, one Chinese, one European — chosen to test whether institutional origin shapes bias.

What we measured

Gender (binary) and race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian) — constrained by what BLS reports, not by what identities matter.

Research questions

RQ1

How do AI-generated personas systematically differ from real U.S. workforce demographics?

RQ2

Do models amplify existing occupational stereotypes, or dampen them?

RQ3

Do bias patterns differ across models from different countries and organizations — or are they shared?

Shift vs. slope decomposition

The regression intercept (α) captures a group's average over/underrepresentation across all jobs. The slope (β) captures whether the model exaggerates or reduces existing skews — a slope above 1.0 means more stereotyping, below 1.0 means less.

Entropy analysis

Shannon entropy measures how spread-out a distribution is. A model generating only male nurses has zero entropy; the real workforce has high entropy. We compare model entropy to BLS entropy for each occupation.

Robustness checks

Three regression specifications were used: raw, trimmed (dropping top/bottom 5% of occupations), and robust regression (downweighting high-leverage outliers). Robust regression is the primary specification. All tests use false discovery rate correction.

Limitation: BLS as baseline

BLS data is the comparison point, not a fairness ideal. It reflects real-world inequities and excludes non-binary identities. Deviating from BLS doesn't automatically mean harm — and matching BLS doesn't mean fairness.
